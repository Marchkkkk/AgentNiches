## IDEA A — memory / AI bottleneck (rides the hottest live trend, named giant Samsung)

TITLE: China Is Outbuilding Samsung 5 to 1 — at the Worst Possible Time
(Note: "5 to 1" = NEW 2026 wafer capacity only, not total output — Samsung stays far larger.
Defuse this in the first 15 seconds of the script, the way Eastern Engine does, so the title
stays honest. Em dash is fine in the TITLE only; the SPOKEN script still bans em dashes.)

Driving question (the puzzle the whole video cracks): why would the world's youngest major memory
maker pour capital into FIVE TIMES Samsung's new wafer capacity at the single most dangerous moment
in memory history — the top of a price cycle that has bankrupted faster-moving firms for decades?

Anchor (verify at script time): CXMT (Changxin Memory) is reportedly adding ~85,000 wafer starts
per month of new DRAM capacity in 2026 vs Samsung's ~15,000 — five times the NEW capacity, not five
times total output (Samsung remains far larger). CXMT sits around 8 percent of global DRAM revenue,
fourth behind Samsung (~38 percent), SK Hynix (~29 percent), and Micron. First-quarter 2026 revenue
reportedly rose more than 700 percent year over year, lifting it from losses into profit, partly on
a memory shortage that AI data centers are driving. CXMT is raising roughly 4.3 billion dollars in a
Shanghai listing and targeting its first high bandwidth memory packaging by end of 2026.

Three answers, escalating (the unravel):
1. Popular answer: they are cashing in on the AI memory shortage while prices are high. Incomplete —
   that is exactly the bet that has punished memory firms for forty years when supply catches demand.
2. Deeper answer: it is a domestic-supply play — China wants its own memory, immune to foreign cutoff.
   Still incomplete — that explains conventional DRAM, but not the rush into high bandwidth memory,
   the hardest, lowest-yield product, where SK Hynix and Nvidia already own the relationship.
3. Real mechanism: capacity installed in 2026 sets the price floor for 2027 and 2028. The bet is not
   on this year's profit, it is on being big enough to MOVE the market when the next shortage hits —
   in a product where two extra percent of supply swings every rival's margins.

The LOCK reading (Jade thesis, fresh wording): the most advanced memory was the thing the incumbents
guarded hardest, so that is precisely the door CXMT is trying to unlock. Read the HBM gap backward
and it tells you what the leaders thought was worth keeping scarce.

Honest turn (both readings half-wrong): bullish — a fourth memory giant capable of moving global
supply. Bearish — building into a cycle peak with unproven yields and a real HBM gap. Synthesis: the
five-times number proves aggression, not leadership; what comes out of the fabs decides which it was.

Reframe-the-question close: the real question was never "is CXMT overtaking Samsung." It is "can it
turn 85,000 monthly wafers into reliable, qualified high bandwidth memory before the shortage ends."
Answer that, and the cycle stops being a graveyard and becomes a moat.

Named giant: Samsung (title) + SK Hynix + Micron + Nvidia (HBM relationship). Demand: memory shortage
+ AI data center = top breakout band. Protagonist/object thread: a single DRAM wafer traced from a
blank disk to a finished memory die.

## IDEA B — "Nobody banned Nvidia from China. Nvidia left anyway." (verified, named giant Nvidia)

TITLE (draft): Nobody Banned Nvidia From China. Nvidia Walked Away First.

Driving question: why would the company that built much of its empire on Chinese demand abandon that
market on its OWN math, before any government forced it out?

Anchor (operator-supplied, verified March 2026; re-confirm at script time): Nvidia reportedly halted
its own H200 production for the China market and redirected that TSMC manufacturing capacity to its
next-generation Vera Rubin chips — not because of a new ban, but because waiting on stalled export
approvals cost more than walking away near-term. Pair with the disclosure angle: Nvidia posted record
fiscal-2026 revenue (reportedly ~216 billion dollars, up ~65 percent) while its China H200 business
sat near zero delivered units — the market it was built on contributing almost nothing to a record year.

Three answers, escalating:
1. Popular answer: it is politics — the export-control fight made China too hard to serve. Incomplete:
   no NEW ban forced this specific halt; Nvidia chose it.
2. Deeper answer: it is scarce-capacity triage — every TSMC wafer spent on a stalled-approval chip is a
   wafer not spent on Vera Rubin, where demand is uncapped. Still incomplete: that explains the
   redirect, not why China stopped being load-bearing at all.
3. Real mechanism: when a market's access is permanently uncertain, its option value collapses to near
   zero in the planning model, and a rational firm stops pricing it in — Nvidia did not lose China, it
   wrote China out of the forecast.

The LOCK reading: the export regime was a lock meant to keep advanced chips OUT of China; read it
backward and it also locked Nvidia's own capacity away from a market it could no longer count on. The
lock cut both ways.

Honest turn: bullish for China — a domestic-accelerator opening as the foreign leader exits. Bearish —
losing the frontier chip widens the software-and-tooling gap, not closes it. Synthesis: an exit creates
room, it does not hand anyone the capability that left with it.

Reframe-the-question close: the real question was never "who banned Nvidia." It is "what does a market
become once the leader stops counting on it" — for the buyer and the seller both.

Named giant: Nvidia (title). Demand: china chip war keyword + Nvidia-vs-China. Protagonist/object
thread: one H200-class accelerator's allocated TSMC wafer, traced from the China order book to the
Vera Rubin line.
